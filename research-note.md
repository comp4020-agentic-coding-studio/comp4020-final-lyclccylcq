# Building a Workflow Around AI Coding

If I had to say what changed the most for me in 2026, my answer would definitely be AI agents. As a student with a software-engineering background, I have had to rethink which parts of development need my attention. After ten months of learning and practice, I think I have my own answer: a whole new workflow, with AI doing much of the implementation, developers making decisions, and verification and testing at its centre. That includes deciding who does the checking and who carries its cost.

Software engineering already has TDD, review and CI, so why call this new? [Nagappan et al.](https://research.ibm.com/publications/realizing-quality-improvement-through-test-driven-development-results-and-experiences-of-four-industrial-teams) reported lower defect density and longer initial development time in four industrial TDD teams. These methods still matter. I want AI to improve mature engineering methods by changing where developers spend attention. A harness should give agents clear boundaries and checks, while keeping someone responsible for accepting the work.

[Cui et al.](https://demirermert.github.io/Papers/Demirer_AI_productivity.pdf) found increased task completion in three field experiments with a code-completion assistant. [He et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01904v2), observing one company, reported more PR output alongside more reviewer work. Together, they make me ask how much output reaches an acceptable result once review and rework count. Faster generation makes requirement breakdown and acceptance more important.

People discuss prompt engineering, but I think important work happens earlier: deciding what we want and how to check it. In four game applications, [Villamizar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29208) found requirement-quality problems generally associated with more generated-code test failures. Clear requirements help, but cannot replace verification.

Testing shows both AI's progress and its risk. Meta's [TestGen-LLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09171) generates test improvements, then filters for successful builds, reliable execution and increased coverage. It protects existing behaviour; a person must decide whether that behaviour is wanted. Code feels like a "black box" when I lose track of its assumptions. Tests from the same misunderstanding could agree with the code and still miss the requirement. [Google's review guidance](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/looking-for.html) asks whether tests are useful and fail when code breaks. I need to understand what passing means.

The mathematics debate makes this a social problem as well. [Henry Cohn](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/09/15/the-technical-debt-of-ai-generated-mathematics/) describes AI companies and individuals claiming success while leaving mathematicians to understand and examine poorly explained output. The [Association for Human Mathematics](https://www.ahmath.org/statements) later urged mathematicians to stop working with OpenAI, calling its mass release contrary to research norms. I read these objections as raising a question about who gets the credit and whose expert time is taken for granted. [Godot](https://godotengine.org/article/contribution-policy-2026/) describes a similar software problem: cheaper contributions without more reviewers, and responds by substantially restricting AI use. I prefer to improve the workflow, but I agree that generators cannot simply leave others to absorb the cost. A company or contributor claiming a productivity gain should count the work left for the people receiving its output.

I expect AI reviewing AI to matter, but a new black box approving the original black box does not reassure me. [OpenAI's practitioner report](https://alignment.openai.com/scaling-code-verification/) describes better review with repository context and execution, while stressing false-alarm costs. [Jin and Chen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00539) find models wrongly rejecting correct implementations in programming benchmarks; more explanation sometimes worsens this. I take the report as a reason to give reviewers tools, and the benchmark failures as a reason to demand evidence from them: the relevant code, the requirement it breaks and how to reproduce the problem. A long explanation or shell-command trail still needs turning into something a person can follow.

Does review cost cancel out faster production? I do not think this means giving up AI coding. During iteration, we can choose relevant checks and expand them when shared assumptions change. Broader checks before committing or releasing provide the final gate. The point is to control checking costs while keeping responsibility with the people directing the work.

Developers maintain this workflow. I often study other things while an agent works, so my [project rules](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-lyclccylcq/blob/0b8df75d7ecd0870ebc4d8681b26158fd329d9f0/CLAUDE.md#before-calling-something-done) reserve local voice-browser tests for voice changes while retaining pre-commit non-voice checks and full CI. Rules should fit our working habits and improve after mistakes. AI is a tool; the workflow should make it easier to work with. I want the room to explore as a "developer + product manager" that this can create. I care more about whether a product has its own ideas and whether anyone needs it.

For the field, good agentic development should make verification work and its ownership part of the workflow. Teams directing generation should prepare understandable, reproducible work before asking another team or community to accept it. Reviewers need clear acceptance conditions and the ability to refuse unprepared output. AI's productivity gain should include the whole path to acceptance. It should give developers more room to think without quietly spending someone else's time.
