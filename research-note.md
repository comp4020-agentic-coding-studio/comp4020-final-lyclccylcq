# Building a Workflow Around AI Coding

If I had to say what changed the most for me in 2026, my answer would definitely be AI agents. As a student with a software-engineering background, I have had to rethink which parts of development need my attention. After ten months of learning and practice, I think I have my own answer: a whole new workflow, with AI doing much of the implementation, developers making decisions, and verification and testing at its centre.

There is a reasonable objection: software engineering already has TDD, review and CI. Why call this new? [Nagappan et al.](https://research.ibm.com/publications/realizing-quality-improvement-through-test-driven-development-results-and-experiences-of-four-industrial-teams) reported lower defect density and longer initial development time in four industrial TDD teams. These methods still matter. I want AI to improve mature engineering methods by changing where developers spend attention and who accepts the work. The harness needs clear task boundaries, checks and rules that improve after failures.

[Cui et al.](https://demirermert.github.io/Papers/Demirer_AI_productivity.pdf) found increased task completion in three field experiments with a code-completion assistant. [He et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.01904v2), observing one company, reported more PR output alongside more reviewer work. Together, they make me ask how much output reaches an acceptable result once review and rework count. Faster generation makes requirement breakdown and acceptance more important.

People discuss prompt engineering, but I think important work happens earlier: deciding what we want, how to build it and how to check it. In four game applications, [Villamizar et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.29208) found requirement-quality problems generally associated with more generated-code test failures. Clear requirements give AI a better starting point, without guaranteeing correctness.

Testing shows both AI's progress and its risk. Meta's [TestGen-LLM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09171) generates test improvements, then filters for building, reliable execution and increased coverage. It protects existing behaviour; a person must decide whether that behaviour is wanted. AI-generated code can feel like a "black box" when I lose track of its assumptions. Tests written from the same misunderstanding could agree with the code and still miss the requirement. [Google's review guidance](https://google.github.io/eng-practices/review/reviewer/looking-for.html) asks whether tests are useful and fail when code breaks. A passing test helps only if I understand what it checked.

Verification cost also raises a responsibility question. [Henry Cohn](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2026/09/15/the-technical-debt-of-ai-generated-mathematics/) describes poorly explained mathematics leaving others the work of understanding it. [Godot](https://godotengine.org/article/contribution-policy-2026/) describes cheaper contributions without more reviewers, and responds by substantially restricting AI use. I would address this through the workflow: whoever directs generation should prepare work that others can check. Other people's time still counts as a cost.

I expect AI reviewing AI to matter, but a new black box approving the original black box does not reassure me. [OpenAI's practitioner report](https://alignment.openai.com/scaling-code-verification/) describes better review with repository context and code execution, while stressing false-alarm costs. [Jin and Chen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.00539) find models wrongly rejecting correct implementations in programming benchmarks; more explanation sometimes worsens this. I take the report as a reason to give reviewers tools, and the benchmark failures as a reason to demand evidence from them. I want the relevant code, the requirement it breaks and how to reproduce the problem. A long explanation or shell-command trail still needs turning into something a person can follow.

Does review cost cancel out faster production? I do not think this means giving up AI coding. During iteration, we can select checks relevant to the change, expanding them when shared assumptions change. Before committing or releasing, broader checks provide the final gate. The workflow should control review cost while still delivering usable work.

Developers also maintain the workflow. I often study other things while an agent works, so I restricted local voice-browser tests to voice changes to avoid microphone and speaker interruptions. The [project rules](https://github.com/comp4020-agentic-coding-studio/comp4020-final-lyclccylcq/blob/0b8df75d7ecd0870ebc4d8681b26158fd329d9f0/CLAUDE.md#before-calling-something-done) retain pre-commit non-voice checks and full CI. Every developer has working habits; the workflow should fit them. AI is a tool, and I want it to be easier to work with. Fixing a mistake should lead us to ask whether a requirement, test or rule needs updating, so we do not keep explaining the same problem.

That leaves more room for exploration. I increasingly feel like a "developer + product manager". Those familiar demo projects appeal to me less when implementing them no longer has such a high technical barrier. I care more about whether a product has its own ideas and whether anyone needs it. A good workflow should let AI take on implementation, keep verification alongside it, and give developers more attention for those decisions.
